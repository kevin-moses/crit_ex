Masks for Our Digital Lives

Introduction

The internet has evolved from a small network of static webpages to the defining way humans and machines communicate today.

The past decade has shown how digital systems on the net can be hostile to humans. Many organizations and several researchers have defined taxonomies categorizing different types of cyber harms spanning social, physical, digital, economic, psychological, and reputational effects.

The potential for increasingly common and severe cyber harm to humans has risen dramatically with AI technology. Harvard SEAS researchers shows new AI voice phishing attacks were over 16% effective (double that for phishing scams with a relative in distress), and the FBI has seen tens of thousands of complaints regarding AI scams, estimated at ~$893 million in losses last year.

According to Axios, a hacker from China allegedly used AI tools from China-developed models, including DeepSeek, to steal data from tens of thousands of bank customers. The attacker used Claude Code to ask for help finding places to sell the stolen data, according to cybersecurity firm CrowdStrike.

This guide functions as a usable playbook for masking your digital presence. In particular, it provides an actionable manual for the following concerns:

  1. How can I prevent digital systems from capturing and disseminating my information?
  2. What are steps I can take right now to prevent future AI agent threat actors with advanced capabilities from using said data to inflict harm on me and my loved ones?

Systems Map of Threat Actor Capabilities

This systems diagram shows how the capabilities of threat actors has evolved over time. Attackers continue to leverage existing scam techniques like social engineering and data compilation from online and offline sources. In recent years, threat actors can now use data breaches and brokers to harvest informaiton via automated scraping systems. This has led to hyper-personalized phishing, which involves multiple attack vectors through precise social engineering by mimicing trusted people to gain access.

Combined with the effectiveness of modern AI systems, autonomous agent scams are becoming more commonplace, which has led to the rise of voice-based phishing attacks. Services like Deepgram, ElevenLabs, and other speech-to-text or text-to-speech models help create realistic voices to conduct these scams.

Data Broker Removal Strategies

Regardless if some of your data has already been leaked, removing your information from data brokers will safeguard yourself for the future.

Data brokers collect your name, address history, phone numbers and relatives, then sell them to anyone, including other tech companies. This information can get into the hands of threat actors, and provides the raw material for AI scams. Malicious attackers now have an email target for phishing, and a phone target for voice-based scams.

Most brokers will delete you if you ask. The steps below combine two free guides: the Personal Data Removal Workbook by Michael Bazzell of IntelTechniques, and Yael Grauer’s Big Ass Data Broker Opt-Out List, adapted under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Where both cover a broker, the newer steps are used. Opt-out links change often; if one is dead, look for an opt-out or privacy link on the broker’s site.

Check off a broker once you have confirmed your listing is gone. Use its notes to record when you asked and what they said back. Brokers often list people again later, so recheck every few months. Your progress is saved in this browser only.

California residents can use the state’s Delete Request and Opt-Out Platform to reach hundreds of registered brokers with one free request.

I have been unsuccessful in removing my personal information from your website. Per the information provided from your legal privacy policy, please remove the following details from your service.

Full name, physical address, and, only if they appear on your service, telephone number and email address.

Fill these in once and every email link adds them to the message. Write them the way the brokers list you. Leave phone and email blank unless a broker shows them; you can delete lines before sending. Saved in this browser only and never sent anywhere except in the emails you choose to send.

Freezes & Opt-Outs

Removing listings makes you harder to find. These steps make the data that already leaked less useful, since an attacker who has your details still can’t open credit in your name, find you through a search engine, or match your face to your photos.

Notes are saved unencrypted in this browser, so never put freeze PINs or passwords in them.

Freeze your credit

Credit freezes prevent any new accounts or lines of credit from being opened in your name. It helps prevent identity theft and financial fraud, and is completely free. Each bureau needs your name, address, date of birth and Social Security number.
